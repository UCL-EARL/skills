# Universal Survey Writing and Audit Guide

## Purpose

This file guides agents that draft, revise, or audit an academic survey. It is
intended to improve scientific rigor, global coherence, and reader experience
without imposing a fixed story, taxonomy, outline, writing style, or venue.

Treat these rules as decision principles, not a prose template. Adapt them to
the topic, evidence, authors' intent, and target venue. Do not rewrite a
manuscript merely to make it resemble this document.

## Priority Order

When objectives conflict, use this order:

1. factual and evidential correctness;
2. global logical consistency;
3. clarity for the intended reader;
4. explanatory and synthetic value;
5. concision and removal of genuine redundancy;
6. sentence-level polish and formatting.

Never improve flow by changing the science. Never preserve detail merely
because it already exists. Never trade a necessary qualification for a cleaner
claim, but do not bury every claim under defensive caveats.

## Working Modes

### Drafting Mode

- Establish the research question, scope, comparison unit, narrative backbone,
  and section responsibilities before optimizing wording.
- Write substantive sections before polishing the Abstract, Introduction, and
  Conclusion when that better reflects the evidence.
- Uncertainty may be recorded in private notes or explicit draft markers.
- Do not invent a confident answer to fill a gap.
- Temporary repetition is acceptable while ideas are being developed, but it
  must be resolved before publication.

### Revision Mode

- Treat every local edit as a possible global change.
- Search for all definitions, claims, numbers, labels, captions, tables, and
  cross-references affected by the edit.
- Repair the canonical statement and its dependants together instead of adding
  local disclaimers.
- Preserve claims and evidence when moving or compressing prose.

### Final-Audit Mode

- Read the manuscript as a publication, not as a record of how it was written.
- Remove internal workflow language, temporary markers, stale alternatives,
  and author-facing tables.
- Verify the source package as well as the rendered PDF.
- Do not declare the manuscript ready merely because it compiles.

## Establish a Manuscript Source of Truth

Before substantial writing or revision, identify the current canonical choices:

- the central research question and one-sentence thesis;
- the motivation and the gap the survey addresses;
- the scope boundary and comparison unit;
- the role of any definition, framework, taxonomy, standard, or lens;
- the primary organizing framework and any supporting descriptors;
- the corpus boundary, source roles, and temporal cutoff;
- canonical terminology, capitalization, and acronym expansions;
- contribution claims and their permitted strength;
- quantitative denominators and the data source from which they are derived;
- the intended audience and target venue, if known.

If these choices are missing or conflict, do not silently choose among them
when the choice would materially change the paper. Surface the conflict and ask
the authors. Once a choice is made, update every dependent location.

## Maintain One Narrative Backbone

A strong survey should be reducible to a traceable argument:

> problem -> gap -> organizing idea -> literature synthesis -> supported
> findings -> implications and limitations

Every top-level section needs a distinct contract:

- what question it answers;
- what new information or reasoning it contributes;
- what evidence it uses;
- what the next section can assume after reading it.

The manuscript may use several analytical views, but readers should know which
one is primary. Supporting objects, evidence attributes, implementation
patterns, or secondary dimensions must help interpret the primary framework,
not compete with it as undeclared taxonomies.

Transitions should express a real logical dependency. Do not use a transition
to disguise an abrupt topic change. Do not repeat the previous section merely
to create the appearance of continuity.

## Introduce Concepts for Readers, Not for the Authors

- Give an intuitive explanation before a dense operational definition.
- Explain why the concept is useful and what existing vocabulary does not make
  easy to compare; avoid presenting renaming alone as a contribution.
- Use a concrete running example when it genuinely clarifies boundaries or
  responsibility allocation. Do not construct an artificial example solely to
  force every concept into one scenario.
- Define a term before relying on it. Reuse the same term for the same concept.
- Prefer established vocabulary when it is adequate. Introduce new vocabulary
  only when it provides a real analytical distinction.
- State the reading order when several dimensions are unavoidable.
- Avoid introducing many tests, objects, actor roles, evidence levels, and
  exceptions in one paragraph.

Reader load is a design constraint. A scientifically correct framework can
still fail if readers must remember too many parallel axes before seeing what
they explain.

## Control Redundancy Without Damaging Readability

Not every repeated term is redundant. Distinguish:

- **necessary consistency:** stable technical terms, brief reminders, and
  concise cross-references;
- **progressive development:** a concept is introduced, formalized, applied,
  and later interpreted at increasing depth;
- **true redundancy:** repeated motivation, definition, result, caveat, or
  paper inventory that adds no new function.

For any apparently repeated passage, ask:

1. Does it add a definition, evidence, inference, boundary, or transition?
2. Is this the location where the reader needs it?
3. Can a short cross-reference preserve the function?
4. If it is removed, will the section remain understandable on first reading?

Keep the strongest complete version in its natural home. Elsewhere, retain only
the minimum reminder needed for local comprehension.

The major front- and back-matter sections have different jobs:

- **Abstract:** problem, gap, contribution, principal synthesis, and implication.
- **Introduction:** motivation, why now, intuitive concept, scope, research
  questions, contributions, and roadmap.
- **Definition or framework section:** precise semantics, boundaries, and
  analytical use.
- **Comparison or results section:** observable findings grounded in the corpus.
- **Synthesis or discussion section:** interpretation, relationships, tensions,
  and attribution.
- **Research agenda:** open questions and plausible study directions, not a
  disguised second standard.
- **Conclusion:** answers the central question and states the durable takeaway;
  it should not become a list of disclaimers or repeat the full Introduction.

## Protect Global Logical Consistency

Audit distinctions that are frequently collapsed accidentally:

- functional membership vs. focal-corpus eligibility;
- system capability vs. paper-level contribution;
- analytical framework vs. implementation architecture or temporal pipeline;
- taxonomy category vs. descriptive attribute;
- implemented mechanism vs. exercised mechanism;
- interface presence vs. operational authority;
- component contribution vs. integrated-system outcome;
- physical evidence vs. causal isolation vs. formal assurance;
- within-episode adaptation vs. persistent cross-task change;
- missing source detail vs. evidence that a mechanism is absent.

Also verify:

- definitions do not change across Abstract, Introduction, body, Appendix, and
  Conclusion;
- necessary and sufficient conditions are not exchanged;
- actor roles remain stable, including the role of humans;
- analytical sequences are not described elsewhere as rigid chronological
  pipelines;
- examples satisfy the definition they are used to illustrate;
- exclusions do not contradict included cases;
- counts use the same denominator and unit of analysis;
- future-work proposals are not reported as present findings;
- limitations do not invalidate stronger claims made earlier.

When a local change affects any item above, perform a full-manuscript search and
reread the affected section transitions. Do not solve a contradiction by
adding a sentence that merely explains away the inconsistency.

## Match Claim Strength to Evidence

Treat claim strength as a ladder:

1. a source describes or implements a mechanism;
2. an evaluation exercises the mechanism;
3. an integrated system containing the mechanism reports an outcome;
4. a matched contrast associates variation in the mechanism with an outcome;
5. a suitable design supports a component-level causal claim;
6. broad necessity, universality, prevalence, or field-wide trend claims require
   correspondingly broad evidence.

Do not jump between levels.

- Without an appropriate controlled contrast, attribute performance to the
  evaluated integrated system.
- Replace unsupported necessity language with a normative requirement of the
  proposed framework or with the narrower observation that reviewed systems
  expose, motivate, or support a concern.
- Do not infer the absence of a mechanism from incomplete reporting.
- Do not turn a selected corpus distribution into field prevalence.
- Treat `first`, `only`, `major trend`, and universal claims as high-risk. Verify
  them directly, scope them precisely, and state them only where they matter.
- Separate what the survey proposes from what the literature has established.
- Phrase hypotheses and research directions as testable questions or candidate
  study designs unless evidence already supports stronger conclusions.

Citation proximity is not citation validity. Each citation must support the
specific clause attached to it, using the correct source version and system
identity. Do not cite a related paper as evidence for an unevaluated component
or later system version. Never fabricate bibliographic metadata, results,
baselines, or evaluation details.

## Build Credible Frameworks and Taxonomies

A survey may propose its own taxonomy, framework, terminology, or operational
standard. Credibility does not come from pretending that the categories are
natural laws. It comes from:

- a clear analytical purpose;
- operationally distinguishable dimensions;
- explicit scope and neighboring cases;
- consistent application across representative literature;
- useful findings that would be obscured by paper-by-paper narration;
- sensitivity to reasonable boundary choices;
- evidence that readers can inspect;
- modest claims about external validation.

Avoid adding a new axis whenever a difficult case appears. If several axes are
needed, identify one primary organizing framework and explain the supporting
role of the rest. A framework applied by its authors demonstrates usefulness
and internal consistency; it does not by itself establish independent external
validation or universal reproducibility.

## Handle Corpus and Review Scope Honestly

- Match the review method to the paper's actual goal. A concept-driven survey,
  recent-wave synthesis, historical review, and systematic review need not use
  identical procedures.
- Do not claim exhaustiveness, prevalence, or systematic coverage without the
  corresponding search and screening design.
- Clearly distinguish focal systems, mechanism or lineage sources, neighboring
  work, and contextual surveys.
- Record stable source identities, version relationships, and temporal cutoff
  when the literature changes rapidly.
- Explain scope and limitations as scholarly choices, not as a chronology of
  internal screening decisions.
- Reproducibility material should help readers inspect sources and evidence; it
  should not expose private deliberation or unfinished candidate lists.

## Write Positive, Precise, Reader-Friendly Prose

- Lead a paragraph with its substantive point, then state the evidence boundary.
- Prefer one main paragraph job: motivation, definition, evidence, inference,
  limitation, or transition.
- Use concrete nouns and active verbs. Avoid long chains of abstract nouns.
- Break long citation inventories into analytical groups.
- Avoid several consecutive sentences that only say what the paper, framework,
  or evidence does not do.
- Preserve uncertainty, but integrate it where the claim is made instead of
  accumulating disclaimer paragraphs.
- Do not use inflated novelty language to compensate for a weak distinction.
- Expand unfamiliar acronyms at first use and avoid acronyms used only once.
- Keep capitalization, hyphenation, singular/plural use, and role labels stable.

Do not write like a patch log. Readers should encounter a coherent argument,
not a sequence of defensive sentences added after anticipated reviewer attacks.

## Use Tables, Figures, and Appendices for Readers

Every main-text table or figure should answer a specific reader-facing question
that prose cannot answer as clearly.

- Keep only synthesis, comparison, or explanatory views needed to follow the
  argument in the main text.
- Move detailed paper-level evidence to an Appendix or Supplementary Material
  when it is necessary for inspection but interrupts the narrative.
- Remove author-facing ledgers, unfinished screens, and bookkeeping tables from
  publication-facing material.
- Do not reproduce the same information in a main-text table, Appendix table,
  and surrounding prose.
- Introduce each table or figure before it appears and interpret its main
  message afterward.
- Captions should state the object, comparison, and important scope boundary.
- If every cell needs paragraph-length caveats, redesign or relocate the table.
- Check visual density, line breaks, font size, whitespace, and continuation
  headers in the compiled PDF, not only in source.

Appendices are still part of the publication. They should read as scholarly
support, not as an internal codebook.

## Keep Internal Workflow out of Publication-Facing Material

Do not expose private process artifacts in the manuscript, Appendix,
Supplementary Material, source comments, or submission archive unless they are
deliberately converted into a reader-facing method or evidence artifact.

Examples of material that normally remains internal:

- drafting plans, decision logs, debate transcripts, and agent instructions;
- internal codebooks, coder packages, adjudication notes, and confidence labels;
- search scratchpads, candidate screens, reading cards, and claim ledgers;
- statements about absent human involvement or automated drafting/coding;
- `TODO`, `FIXME`, placeholder text, stale alternatives, and commented-out
  claims;
- private paths, credentials, tokens, unpublished links, and local metadata.

This rule does not prohibit transparent review methodology. Describe the final
method, source roles, criteria, evidence semantics, and limitations directly,
without narrating the private chronology by which the authors reached them.
It also does not override required disclosures about AI assistance, conflicts,
data, code, or review methods; follow the current venue policy and place each
required disclosure in its designated location.

Create a clean submission allowlist. Never upload an entire working repository
or Overleaf export without checking what it contains.

## Protocol for Every Substantive Edit

1. **Locate the root issue.** Determine whether it is factual, conceptual,
   structural, evidential, terminological, or presentational.
2. **Map dependencies.** Find every definition, claim, count, table, caption,
   Appendix entry, and conclusion that depends on it.
3. **Choose the canonical resolution.** If the choice changes scope,
   contribution, corpus, taxonomy, or claim strength, confirm it with the
   authors.
4. **Edit the smallest coherent set.** Avoid both a one-sentence patch and an
   unnecessary rewrite.
5. **Reread locally.** Check the full paragraph, subsection, and neighboring
   transitions rather than only the changed lines.
6. **Audit globally.** Search for old terminology, stale numbers, contradictory
   formulations, and repeated caveats.
7. **Validate evidence.** Confirm that citations and comparisons still support
   the revised wording.
8. **Compile and inspect.** Check references, tables, figures, typography, and
   page flow in the rendered document.
9. **Report uncertainty.** Do not guess when evidence or authorial intent remains
   materially ambiguous.

## When the Agent Should Stop and Ask

Ask the authors before making a change that would materially alter:

- the central thesis, title, scope, or intended audience;
- the primary framework, taxonomy, or definition;
- inclusion or exclusion of a paper from the focal corpus;
- a contribution or novelty claim;
- a quantitative denominator or derived finding;
- the interpretation of conflicting sources;
- the balance between scientific qualification and intended rhetorical stance;
- publication identity, anonymity, licensing, or venue compliance.

Do not ask about minor wording when a safe, evidence-preserving edit is clear.

## Final Audit Sequence

Run separate passes. A single reread rarely catches all failure modes.

### Pass 1: Story and Section Ownership

- Can the thesis be stated in one sentence?
- Does every section have a unique job?
- Does the order follow the reader's information needs?
- Are transitions logically earned?

### Pass 2: Definitions and Conceptual Load

- Are key terms intuitive before they become technical?
- Is one framework visibly primary?
- Are supporting dimensions necessary and clearly subordinate?
- Can a non-specialist reader explain the concept, boundary, and purpose?

### Pass 3: Global Consistency and Contradiction

- Do scope, definition, contribution, and limitation match everywhere?
- Are actor roles, units of analysis, timelines, and denominators stable?
- Do examples, tables, and appendices implement the same rules as the prose?

### Pass 4: Redundancy and Reader Burden

- Does every paragraph add a new function?
- Are repeated definitions replaced by concise reminders where appropriate?
- Are paper inventories organized into findings or mechanism families?
- Are caveats concentrated at the claims they bound?

### Pass 5: Claims, Evidence, and Citations

- Is every claim no stronger than its evidence?
- Are integrated-system outcomes attributed correctly?
- Are novelty, necessity, trend, and causal claims verified?
- Do citations support the precise adjacent clause and correct source version?

### Pass 6: Tables, Figures, and Appendices

- Is each visual necessary and readable?
- Does surrounding prose explain its purpose and takeaway?
- Is detailed evidence available without duplicating the main narrative?
- Is all publication-facing supplementary material reader-oriented?

### Pass 7: Language and Accessibility

- Are paragraphs focused and sentences readable?
- Are terminology, capitalization, acronyms, and hyphenation consistent?
- Is the tone confident but scientifically bounded rather than defensive?
- Have unnecessary abstraction and jargon been removed?

### Pass 8: Submission Hygiene

- Does the paper build from a clean allowlisted source package?
- Are citations, references, labels, and bibliography entries resolved?
- Are author identity and metadata correct for the submission mode?
- Are venue template, page limit, Appendix, Supplementary Material, and
  licensing requirements verified from current official instructions?
- Are internal files, comments, credentials, build artifacts, and stale drafts
  excluded?

## Completion Criteria

A survey is ready for author approval when a reader can answer:

1. What phenomenon or literature does the survey study?
2. Why is a new synthesis useful now?
3. What is inside and outside the scope?
4. What organizing framework does the survey contribute or adopt?
5. What does the literature collectively reveal?
6. Which conclusions are directly supported, and where are the evidence limits?
7. What should researchers or practitioners do differently after reading it?

The manuscript should also have:

- no unresolved global contradiction;
- no unsupported causal, universal, prevalence, or novelty claim;
- no genuine paragraph-level redundancy;
- no unnecessary conceptual axis competing with the primary framework;
- no internal workflow artifact in publication-facing files;
- no mismatch between prose, tables, appendices, counts, and citations;
- a reproducible, venue-appropriate submission package.

Passing this checklist does not guarantee acceptance. It ensures that the
survey presents its actual contribution coherently, rigorously, and accessibly
without asking readers to reconstruct the authors' reasoning or workflow.
